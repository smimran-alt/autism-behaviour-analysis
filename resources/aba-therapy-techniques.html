ABA Therapy Techniques: DTT, NET, Prompting and Reinforcement

Understand common Applied Behaviour Analysis teaching strategies, how they differ, and how they can support meaningful communication, learning and independence.

For parents, caregivers, educators and ABA professionals.

What are ABA teaching techniques?

Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA) uses principles of learning and behaviour to understand how environments influence skills and actions. ABA teaching techniques are structured ways of supporting learning, measuring progress and adjusting support based on individual needs.

Common approaches include Discrete Trial Training (DTT), Natural Environment Teaching (NET), prompting, prompt fading and reinforcement. These approaches can be combined, but they should always be adapted to the learner's communication style, preferences, abilities and goals.

Important: Meaningful goals should prioritize communication, choice, safety, participation and quality of life. Teaching should respect the learner's dignity, preferences and assent, and should not aim to suppress harmless autistic traits simply to make someone appear less autistic.

1. Discrete Trial Training (DTT)

Discrete Trial Training is a structured teaching approach in which a skill is divided into small, clearly defined learning opportunities. A practitioner presents an instruction or opportunity, observes the learner's response and provides appropriate feedback or support.

Example: Identifying an object

  1. Prepare: Place a spoon and a cup where the learner can see and reach them.
  2. Instruction: Say, “Show me the spoon,” if spoken instructions are accessible to the learner.
  3. Response: Allow time for the learner to point, touch, look toward or otherwise identify the spoon using an agreed response method.
  4. Feedback: Acknowledge the response and provide appropriate reinforcement or additional teaching support.
  5. Record: Note whether the response was independent, prompted or not yet demonstrated.

DTT can be useful for teaching specific, measurable skills, especially when the learner benefits from predictable instructions and repeated opportunities. However, practicing a skill at a table does not automatically mean the skill will be used in daily life.

2. Natural Environment Teaching (NET)

Natural Environment Teaching uses everyday activities, interests and naturally occurring opportunities to support learning. Teaching may happen during play, meals, reading, outdoor activities or other routines.

Example: Requesting during play

A child enjoys building a tower. During play, an adult creates an opportunity for the child to request another block. The child might request using speech, a gesture, a picture, a communication device or another accessible method. The adult responds by providing the block and continuing the enjoyable activity.

NET can help connect learning to real situations and support generalization across people, places and activities. It works best when the learner remains actively engaged and their interests and choices guide the interaction.

Neither approach is automatically better for every learner or goal. Teaching decisions should consider the individual's preferences, learning history, access needs and whether the skill transfers into daily life.

3. Prompting and prompt fading

A prompt is additional assistance provided to help a learner respond or complete a skill. Prompts should be chosen thoughtfully, used only as needed and reduced as the learner becomes more independent.

Visual prompts

Pictures, schedules, written instructions or visual examples that clarify what to do next.

Gestural prompts

Pointing or gesturing toward a relevant object, location or action.

Model prompts

Demonstrating an action so the learner can observe how it is performed.

Verbal prompts

Providing an additional spoken cue when spoken language is accessible and useful.

Physical guidance, when considered at all, requires particular attention to consent or assent, safety, professional oversight and less intrusive alternatives. It should never be used to force compliance or override distress.

What is prompt fading?

Prompt fading means gradually reducing extra help so the learner can perform the skill more independently. For example, an adult might initially point to a picture schedule, later use a smaller gesture and eventually allow the learner to check the schedule without a prompt.

Progress should be measured by increased independence and useful participation, not simply by completing more prompted trials.

4. Positive reinforcement

Positive reinforcement occurs when something is added after a behaviour and that consequence increases the likelihood of the behaviour happening again in similar circumstances.

For example, if a learner requests a favorite game and receives access to that game, requesting may become more likely in the future. Whether something functions as reinforcement is determined by its effect on future behaviour, not just whether the adult intended it as a reward.

Examples of possible reinforcers

  • Access to a preferred toy, game or activity
  • Positive social interaction when welcomed by the learner
  • Choosing what happens next
  • A natural outcome, such as receiving a requested item

Reinforcement should be individualized and ethical. Basic needs, access to communication, dignity and necessary accommodations should not be withheld to make someone comply with a task.

5. Measuring progress responsibly

Data collection helps educators, caregivers and professionals evaluate whether teaching strategies are working. Depending on the goal, measures may include independent responses, prompted responses, frequency, duration or successful use of a skill across everyday settings.

Data should be interpreted alongside the learner's comfort, preferences, participation and quality of life. A high percentage of correct responses alone does not establish that an intervention is meaningful or appropriate.

6. Practical tips for parents and caregivers

  • Choose goals that matter to the learner and family.
  • Recognize all accessible forms of communication, including AAC.
  • Build learning opportunities into enjoyable daily routines.
  • Offer genuine choices and respect refusals and signs of distress.
  • Use prompts carefully and plan to reduce unnecessary support.
  • Review progress regularly and change strategies when they are not helping.
  • Work with qualified professionals when developing individualized intervention plans.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between DTT and NET?

DTT uses structured, planned learning trials. NET uses learning opportunities embedded in everyday routines, activities and play. Both can be individualized to support meaningful skills.

Is prompting the same as teaching?

Prompting is one part of teaching. Effective instruction also involves clear goals, opportunities to practice, feedback, generalization and reducing unnecessary assistance over time.

Does positive reinforcement always mean giving treats?

No. Reinforcement can involve preferred activities, social interaction, choices or natural outcomes. Its defining feature is that it increases the future likelihood of the behaviour.

Can ABA techniques support nonspeaking children?

Teaching can support nonspeaking learners when goals and strategies recognize their preferred communication methods, including gestures, pictures, AAC devices and other accessible forms of expression.

Should parents use these techniques without professional guidance?

Parents can use supportive everyday practices such as offering choices, modeling and responding to communication. Individualized or intensive behaviour intervention should be planned and reviewed with appropriately qualified professionals.
